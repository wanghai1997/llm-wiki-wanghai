# 研发微观基础

The microfoundations of R&D explain why market economies produce new knowledge and ideas, and why the **social return to innovation** typically exceeds the **private return**. This gap between social and private benefits is the fundamental market failure that motivates government intervention in research and development.

## Knowledge as an Economic Good

Knowledge and ideas have two distinctive properties that differentiate them from conventional goods:

### 1. Nonrivalry

Knowledge is **nonrival**: once created, an idea can be used by many people simultaneously at zero marginal cost. If one firm uses the Pythagorean theorem, this does not reduce its availability to others.

This means the **marginal cost** of sharing an idea is essentially zero, while the **fixed cost** of creating it (R&D investment) can be very large. The socially optimal price for a nonrival good is zero (marginal cost pricing), but at zero price, private firms cannot recoup their R&D costs.

### 2. Partial Excludability

While ideas are nonrival, they are often **excludable** to some degree:
- **Patents** grant temporary monopoly rights (typically 20 years)
- **Trade secrets** protect proprietary know-how
- **Copyrights** protect creative expressions
- **First-mover advantages** and **learning curves** provide temporary protection

However, excludability is **imperfect**:
- Patents can be "invented around"
- Employees take knowledge to new firms
- Reverse engineering reveals trade secrets
- Academic publication disseminates basic research

## The Social Return vs Private Return Gap

Because knowledge spills over to others, the social return to R&D far exceeds the private return:

| Study | Estimated Social Return | Estimated Private Return | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Griliches (1992) | ~50% per year | ~20% per year | 2.5:1 |
| Jones-Williams (1998) | ~30-50% | ~7-15% | 3-5:1 |
| Bloom et al. (2013) | ~40% per year | ~10% per year | 4:1 |

This massive gap implies that **private markets underinvest in R&D** relative to the social optimum. The optimal policy would subsidize R&D until the marginal social benefit equals marginal cost.

## "Standing on Shoulders" vs "Stepping on Toes"

Innovation involves two countervailing externalities:

### Positive: "Standing on Shoulders" (Knowledge Spillovers)

Today's innovators build on yesterday's discoveries:
- Newton: "If I have seen further, it is by standing on the shoulders of giants"
- Modern researchers cite prior work; patent applications reference earlier patents
- This creates a **positive externality**: innovators do not capture the full social benefit of their discoveries

**Policy implication**: Subsidize R&D to internalize the positive externality.

### Negative: "Stepping on Toes" (Business-Stealing)

Innovators may destroy profits of existing firms:
- A new drug makes an old drug obsolete
- A new technology renders old capital worthless
- This creates a **negative externality**: innovators do not internalize the losses they impose on incumbents

**Policy implication**: The business-stealing effect may lead to **excessive** R&D from a social perspective, partially offsetting the underinvestment from knowledge spillovers.

## Patents as a Policy Instrument

Patents attempt to solve the nonrivalry problem by creating temporary monopoly power:

**Benefits**:
- Provide incentives for R&D investment
- Disclose knowledge publicly (patents must describe the invention)
- Create markets for technology licensing

**Costs**:
- Monopoly pricing creates deadweight loss
- Patent thickets and litigation discourage innovation
- Patent races may duplicate R&D effort
- Patents on basic research may block follow-on innovation

**Optimal patent design** balances these tradeoffs:
- **Length**: Longer patents → stronger incentives but longer monopoly distortions
- **Breadth**: Broader patents → stronger protection but may block follow-on research
- **Height**: Stricter novelty requirements → fewer but higher-quality patents

## Empirical Evidence on R&D Policy

### R&D Tax Credits

Most OECD countries offer tax incentives for R&D:
- **US R&E tax credit** (since 1981): 20% of qualified research expenses above a base amount
- Empirical estimates suggest $1 of tax credit generates $1–2 of additional R&D spending
- However, much of the "additional" R&D may be relabeling of existing activities

### Direct Government R&D

- **Basic research** (NIH, NSF, DARPA in the US): High social returns, low private incentives
- **Applied research** (Solyndra, industrial policy): Risk of government failure—picking losers, political capture
- Evidence: Government-funded basic research has very high social returns; government-funded commercial R&D has mixed results

## 反面论点与数据空白

### [CONTRADICTION] 专利制度的效率
教材将专利呈现为解决知识非排他性的有效工具，但：
- **专利丛林**（patent thicket）：在生物技术、软件等领域，密集的专利网络使创新者面临高额许可成本和诉讼风险
- **专利竞赛**（patent race）：多个企业同时研发同一技术，造成社会资源浪费（"踩脚趾"效应）
- **基础研究的专利化**：Myriad Genetics 对 BRCA 基因的专利、CRISPR 的专利纠纷，显示专利可能阻碍而非促进基础科学进步
- **开源创新**：Linux、Wikipedia、开源软件显示，非专利激励机制（声誉、社区、利他主义）也能产生大量创新

### [BIAS] "更多 R&D = 更好"的线性思维
教材隐含假设更高的 R&D 支出总是 desirable。但：
- **边际回报递减**：随着 R&D 存量增加，新增 R&D 的边际产出可能下降
- **R&D 的构成**：国防 R&D、医药 R&D、软件 R&D 的社会回报差异巨大
- **发展中国家**：最穷国家的问题不是 R&D 不足，而是基本制度、健康和教育。将发达国家的 R&D 政策框架推广到全球是一种误配

### 数据空白
- **创新测度**：如何准确测度 "创新产出"？专利数量是常用代理，但专利质量差异巨大。许多重要创新（新商业模式、组织创新）不获专利
- **中国创新模式**：中国的 R&D 支出已居世界第二，但 "创新" 大量来自渐进式改进和工艺创新，而非原创性突破。这是否符合 Romer 模型的假设？
- **AI 与知识生产**：如果 AI 可以自动化研究和发现，R&D "生产函数"的基本假设（需要人类研究者）可能需要重写。AI 生成的发明是否应获专利？
