# 无代价反通胀

The **costless disinflation** proposition, associated with [[Thomas Sargent]] and the [[理性预期]] revolution, argues that if a central bank can credibly commit to eliminating inflation, the economy can reduce inflation without incurring the usual output losses. Under rational expectations, a credible regime change causes inflation expectations to drop immediately, shifting the short-run Phillips curve downward without requiring a recession.

## The Theoretical Basis

### The Sacrifice Ratio Under Rational Expectations

The [[牺牲率]] measures the cumulative output loss (as a percentage of one year's GDP) required to reduce inflation by 1 percentage point. Under [[适应性预期]], the sacrifice ratio is positive because:
- To reduce inflation, unemployment must rise above the natural rate
- Output falls below potential for an extended period
- Expectations adjust only gradually

Under **rational expectations with credibility**:
- The central bank announces a credible commitment to price stability
- Agents immediately adjust $\pi^e$ downward
- The Phillips curve shifts down without requiring $u > u^n$
- **Sacrifice ratio = 0**

### Sargent's Historical Evidence

[[Thomas Sargent]] (1982, "The Ends of Four Big Inflations") studied the end of hyperinflation in:
1. **Austria** (1922)
2. **Hungary** (1923–24)
3. **Poland** (1923–24)
4. **Germany** (1923)

**Key finding**: In each case, hyperinflation ended abruptly when governments credibly committed to fiscal discipline (balanced budgets, independent central banks). The end of hyperinflation was **not** accompanied by the massive output losses that standard Keynesian models predicted.

Sargent's interpretation: The regime change was so dramatic and credible that expectations adjusted immediately, eliminating the need for a protracted recession.

## The Credibility Requirement

### What Makes a Policy Credible?

For disinflation to be costless, the policy must be:
1. **Clearly communicated**: The public understands the new target and commitment
2. **Institutionally backed**: Legal/structural changes (e.g., central bank independence) make reversal costly
3. **Politically sustainable**: The government can withstand short-term political pressure
4. **Consistent with fiscal policy**: Monetizing deficits must stop

### The Time-Inconsistency Problem

[[Finn Kydland]] and [[Edward Prescott]] (1977) showed that even if a central bank **wants** to commit to low inflation, it has an incentive to renege once expectations have adjusted downward. This **time inconsistency** means that promises alone are not credible—institutional constraints are needed.

## 反面论点与数据空白

### [CONTRADICTION-1] Volcker反通胀的惨痛代价

The most important counterexample to costless disinflation is the **Volcker disinflation** (1979–82):
- Inflation fell from 11.3% (1979) to 3.2% (1983)
- Unemployment rose from 5.8% to **10.8%** (1982)
- Real GDP fell by ~3% in the 1980 and 1982 recessions
- Estimated sacrifice ratio: **~5–10% of GDP per 1% inflation reduction**

Sargent's defense: The Volcker Fed's commitment was **not fully credible** initially. Markets doubted whether the Fed would sustain high interest rates through the recession. Credibility was only established after the severe 1982 recession.

Critics' response: If credibility requires a recession, the theory is **not falsifiable**—any observed output loss can be dismissed as "insufficient credibility."

### [CONTRADICTION-2] 欧洲的"无代价"紧缩?

The European Central Bank maintained price stability throughout the 2010s eurozone crisis:
- Inflation remained near or below 2%
- But unemployment in southern Europe exceeded 20% for years
- Greece, Spain, Portugal experienced **depression-level output losses**

This suggests that **maintaining low inflation is not the same as costlessly achieving it**. The output losses were real and persistent, even if inflation expectations remained anchored.

### [CONTRADICTION-3] 日本的"失去的二十年"

Japan attempted to raise inflation from near-zero to 2% starting in 2013 (Abenomics + QQE):
- The Bank of Japan made an explicit, credible commitment
- Inflation rose only briefly, then fell back
- The "cost" of failing to raise inflation was **not** a recession—but a prolonged period of stagnation

This is the mirror image of costless disinflation: **costless reflation also failed**, suggesting that expectations are not as malleable as rational expectations models assume.

### [CONTRADICTION-4] 制度摩擦与现实调整成本

Even with perfect credibility, real-world frictions make adjustment costly:
- **Wage contracts**: Multi-year contracts prevent immediate wage adjustments
- **Debt deflation**: Falling inflation raises real debt burdens, causing bankruptcies
- **Menu costs**: Price adjustments are costly even when anticipated
- **Hysteresis**: Short-term unemployment can permanently raise the natural rate

These frictions are **not** eliminated by credibility.
