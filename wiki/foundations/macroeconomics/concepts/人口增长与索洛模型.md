# 人口增长与索洛模型

When population grows at rate $n$, the [[Solow增长模型]] must account for the fact that some investment is needed to equip new workers ("capital widening") rather than increasing capital per existing worker ("capital deepening"). This modification yields important predictions about the relationship between population growth and economic prosperity.

## Modified Capital Accumulation Equation

With population growth at rate $n$:

$$\Delta k = sf(k) - (\delta + n)k$$

**Break-even investment** now has two components:
1. **Depreciation replacement**: $\delta k$ (to replace worn-out capital)
2. **Capital widening**: $nk$ (to equip new workers entering the labor force)

The term $(\delta + n)k$ is the total investment required to keep $k$ constant.

## Steady State with Population Growth

The steady state satisfies:

$$sf(k^*) = (\delta + n)k^*$$

### Effect of Higher $n$

A higher population growth rate:
- Raises the break-even investment line (steeper slope)
- **Lowers** the steady-state capital per worker $k^*$
- **Lowers** steady-state output per worker $y^* = f(k^*)$
- **Leaves unchanged** the long-run growth rate of per-capita output (still zero without technological progress)

Intuition: faster population growth means more workers to equip, so less investment per worker is available for capital deepening.

## Comparative Statics with Data

Mankiw presents cross-country evidence showing a negative correlation between population growth and income per person. Countries with high $n$ (e.g., many African nations) tend to have lower $y^*$, consistent with the Solow model's prediction.

However, this correlation does not establish causation:
- **Reverse causality**: Poorer countries may have higher fertility due to lack of education, child mortality, or social safety nets
- **Omitted variables**: Institutional quality, geography, or culture may affect both fertility and productivity
- **Kremerian effects** (see below): Over very long periods, larger populations may accelerate innovation

## [CONTRADICTION] Three Incompatible Population-Growth Theories

Mankiw Ch 8-3 presents three mutually exclusive frameworks in the same section without explaining their respective historical domains:

| Model | Core Mechanism | Population→Income | Historical Domain |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Solow** (Ch 8-3, p.233) | Capital dilution | Higher $n$ → lower $y^*$ | Industrial/post-industrial economies with exogenous technology |
| **Malthus** (Ch 8-3, p.236) | Food constraint | Population growth keeps income at subsistence | Pre-industrial agrarian economies (pre-1800) |
| **Kremer** (Ch 8-3, p.237) | Scale effects in innovation | Larger population → faster tech progress → higher income | Very long run; global scale; modern innovation clusters |

**The textbook's failure**: These three models make opposite predictions and apply to different historical epochs, yet they are presented as coexisting "perspectives" without clear boundaries. A student might reasonably ask: "Should my country encourage or discourage population growth?" and receive three contradictory answers from the same chapter.

### Resolution

The three models are not competing explanations for the same phenomenon; they describe different regimes:

1. **Malthusian regime** (before ~1800): Technology grows slowly; any income above subsistence is eaten up by population growth. Income per capita is stagnant.
2. **Post-Malthusian/Solow regime** (1800–present in developed economies): Technology grows fast enough to outpace population; capital accumulation becomes the binding constraint. Higher fertility dilutes capital per worker.
3. **Kremerian/endogenous growth regime** (very long run, global scale): Population size drives innovation; scale effects dominate. More people → more ideas → faster growth.

Unified growth theory (Galor 2000, 2011) formalizes the transition between these regimes. Mankiw's textbook does not mention unified growth theory.

## Policy Implications

In the Solow framework:
- **Population control policies** (family planning, education for women) can raise steady-state living standards by reducing $n$
- **Immigration policy** involves tradeoffs: immigrants raise total output but may dilute capital per worker in the short run
- However, policies affecting $n$ also have demographic, ethical, and political dimensions that the model ignores

## 反面论点与数据空白

### [CONTRADICTION-2] 适用边界未标注
教材将 Solow、Malthus、Kremer 三种互斥的人口-增长关系并置在同一节（Ch 8-3），却未解释各自的适用边界（前工业时代 vs 工业时代 vs 知识经济时代）。这导致学生无法判断在何种情境下应应用哪种模型。

### [regional-bias] 人口政策的西方视角
教材以欧美低生育率为讨论背景，暗示"人口增长降低人均收入"是普遍规律。但：
- 东亚经济体（韩国、台湾）在高人口增长期经历了快速资本积累和技术追赶
- 非洲的人口增长 debate 涉及殖民遗产、疾病负担、女性教育等复杂因素，不能简化为 Solow 的 $n$ 参数
- 中国的一胎政策是特定历史条件下的极端实验，其经济后果仍存争议

### 数据空白
- **内生生育率**：Becker-Barro 的生育率经济学将子女数量和质量视为家庭最优化选择，人口增长不再是外生参数。教材未涉及
- **人口老龄化**：发达经济体面临 $n < 0$ 的新现实（日本、德国、意大利），Solow 模型对负增长人口的预测（资本 deepening 无限持续）显然有问题
- **气候与人口**：IPCC 框架下，人口增长增加碳排放，但也会加速绿色技术创新（Kremerian 机制）。这种 tension 完全超出教材范围
