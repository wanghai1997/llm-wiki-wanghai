# 粘性价格理论

The **sticky price theory** explains why the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve slopes upward: firms face costs of adjusting prices ("menu costs") and therefore do not immediately respond to changes in aggregate demand. When the price level rises unexpectedly, firms that have not adjusted their prices find their relative prices lower, which increases demand for their products and leads them to increase production.

## The Mechanism

### Menu Costs

Firms face real costs when changing prices:
- **Physical costs**: Reprinting menus, catalogs, and price tags
- **Decision costs**: Managerial time spent analyzing market conditions and setting new prices
- **Customer costs**: Annoying customers with frequent price changes, potentially damaging relationships
- **Coordination costs**: Ensuring consistent pricing across multiple products and locations

Mankiw (1985) showed that even very small menu costs—representing a tiny fraction of firm revenue—can lead to significant price rigidity because the private benefit of adjusting prices may be small relative to the cost.

### The Aggregate Supply Implication

In an economy with many firms, each setting its own price:
- Some fraction of firms adjust prices each period; others keep prices fixed
- When aggregate demand increases (raising the overall price level $P$):
  - Firms that **have** adjusted raise their prices in line with $P$
  - Firms that **have not** adjusted have lower relative prices ($p_i / P$ falls)
  - These firms see increased demand and increase output
- The result: higher aggregate output at a higher price level → upward-sloping SRAS

### The New Keynesian Formalization: Calvo Pricing

[[Guillermo Calvo]] (1983) formalized sticky prices in a tractable dynamic model:
- Each firm has a probability $\theta$ of being able to reset its price in any period
- When resetting, the firm chooses a price based on expected future costs and demand
- Only a fraction $(1-\theta)$ of firms adjust each period
- This yields the **New Keynesian Phillips curve**:

$$\pi_t = \beta E_t\pi_{t+1} + \kappa (y_t - \bar{y}_t)$$

where inflation depends on expected future inflation and the output gap.

## Alternative Microfoundations

### Time-Dependent Pricing
- **Taylor (1980)**: Fixed-duration ("staggered") contracts; firms adjust at predetermined intervals
- **Calvo (1983)**: Random-duration adjustment; each firm has a constant probability of adjusting

### State-Dependent Pricing
- **Golosov-Lucas (2007)**: Firms adjust when the benefit exceeds the menu cost; implies large price changes when they occur
- **Midrigan (2011)**: Multi-product firms face economies of scope in price adjustment

State-dependent models are more realistic but mathematically more complex; Calvo pricing remains the workhorse for policy analysis.

## 反面论点与数据空白

### [CONTRADICTION-1] 价格粘性的程度被夸大

教材将价格粘性作为SRAS的核心解释,但实证证据显示:
- **Bils-Klenow (2004)**:美国消费者价格平均**每4-6个月**调整一次——远短于"几年粘性"的教学叙事
- **Nakamura-Steinsson (2008)**:修正为约9-12个月,但仍短于传统假设
- **疫情期间(2020-22)**:汽车、服务、食品价格在数月内剧烈调整,远快于菜单成本预测

这些发现暗示价格粘性不是均匀现象,而是**依赖冲击的性质与方向**。

### [CONTRADICTION-2] 宏观粘性 ≠ 微观粘性

宏观价格水平调整缓慢不等于微观价格不调整:
- 微观数据:许多价格每月甚至每周调整
- 宏观数据:价格指数变化平滑
- 原因:**加总效应**——部分价格上涨、部分下降,相互抵消;且消费者篮子的权重平滑了波动

教材有时混淆微观和宏观层面的"粘性"。

### [CONTRADICTION-3] 线上经济的价格灵活性

电子商务和算法定价正在改变价格粘性:
- **Amazon**:价格可能一天调整多次
- **动态定价**:机票、酒店、网约车使用实时算法定价
- **在线比较购物**:降低消费者搜索成本,迫使企业更频繁调整价格

在数字经济中,菜单成本趋近于零,价格粘性理论可能 increasingly 不适用。

### 数据空白

1. **行业差异**:服务业价格调整远快于制造业,但教材将"价格粘性"作为同质假设
2. **冲击方向不对称**:价格上升比下降更容易——教科书 symmetric 处理不准确
3. **预期的作用**:前瞻性定价(Calvo)意味着当前通胀取决于未来预期,但教材的静态分析未充分讨论
4. **中国的价格粘性**:计划经济遗产+行政干预使价格调整机制与市场经济不同
