# 资本积累与稳态

**Capital accumulation and the steady state** are the core mechanical components of the [[Solow增长模型]]. This page explains the investment-depreciation process, the definition and properties of the steady state, and the economy's transition dynamics.

## The Capital Accumulation Equation

In the Solow model, the change in per-worker capital stock equals investment minus depreciation:

$$\Delta k = sf(k) - \delta k$$

### Components

**Actual investment** ($sf(k)$):
- A constant fraction $s$ of output per worker is saved and invested
- Because of diminishing returns ($f''(k) < 0$), the investment curve is concave in $k$
- As $k \to 0$, $f'(k) \to \infty$ (Inada condition), so investment rises very steeply

**Break-even investment** ($\delta k$):
- Capital wears out at rate $\delta$
- To keep $k$ constant, investment must exactly replace depreciated capital
- The break-even line is straight from the origin with slope $\delta$

## The Steady State

The **steady state** $k^*$ is the point where $\Delta k = 0$:

$$sf(k^*) = \delta k^*$$

### Properties of the Steady State

1. **Existence**: Given Inada conditions, the curves $sf(k)$ and $\delta k$ must cross at some $k^* > 0$
2. **Uniqueness**: Because $sf(k)$ is concave and $\delta k$ is linear, there is exactly one positive steady state
3. **Stability**: If $k < k^*$, $\Delta k > 0$ (capital accumulates); if $k > k^*$, $\Delta k < 0$ (capital decumulates). The economy converges to $k^*$ from any starting point

### Steady-State Output and Consumption

At $k^*$:
- Output per worker: $y^* = f(k^*)$
- Consumption per worker: $c^* = (1-s)f(k^*) = f(k^*) - \delta k^*$
- Investment per worker: $i^* = sf(k^*) = \delta k^*$

## Numerical Example (Mankiw Table 8-2)

Consider a Cobb-Douglas production function $y = k^{1/2}$ with $s = 0.3$ and $\delta = 0.05$:

| Period | $k$ | $y = k^{1/2}$ | $c = 0.7y$ | $i = 0.3y$ | $\delta k$ | $\Delta k$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4.000 | 2.000 | 1.400 | 0.600 | 0.200 | 0.400 |
| 2 | 4.400 | 2.098 | 1.468 | 0.629 | 0.220 | 0.409 |
| ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| $\infty$ | 36.000 | 6.000 | 4.200 | 1.800 | 1.800 | 0.000 |

Steady-state $k^* = (s/\delta)^2 = (0.3/0.05)^2 = 36$.

The economy converges gradually: each period's capital increase is smaller than the last, following a path of **diminishing transitional growth**.

## Transition Dynamics

### Speed of Convergence

The economy approaches $k^*$ at a rate that depends on how far $k$ is from $k^*$. Near the steady state, the convergence speed is approximately:

$$\frac{d(\ln k)}{dt} \approx \lambda (\ln k^* - \ln k)$$

where $\lambda = (1-\alpha_K)(n + g + \delta)$ for a Cobb-Douglas production function with capital share $\alpha_K$. Empirically, this implies convergence at roughly **2% per year**—a half-life of about 35 years.

### Implications

1. **History matters temporarily**: Countries starting with $k_0 \ll k^*$ experience a period of rapid growth as they catch up to their steady state
2. **Policy changes have delayed effects**: An increase in $s$ raises $k^*$, but it takes decades for the economy to approach the new steady state
3. **Short-run growth can be high even without technological progress**: During transition, capital deepening alone drives per-capita growth

## Extensions

When population grows at rate $n$ (see [[人口增长与索洛模型]]), break-even investment becomes $(\delta + n)k$. When technology grows at rate $g$ (see [[技术进步与索洛模型]]), the effective capital stock evolves with break-even investment $(\delta + n + g)\tilde{k}$. The qualitative properties—existence, uniqueness, stability of steady state—remain unchanged.

## 反面论点与数据空白

### [CONTRADICTION] Solow vs Real-World Capital Dynamics
The Solow model assumes capital adjusts smoothly and instantaneously to its steady state. In reality:
- **Adjustment costs**: Installing new capital takes time and resources; firms face convex costs of rapid investment
- **Financing constraints**: Many firms (especially in developing countries) cannot borrow freely to fund optimal investment
- **Irreversibility**: Capital is often firm-specific or industry-specific, making disinvestment costly
- **Bubbles and financial cycles**: Asset price bubbles can cause capital to deviate systematically from fundamentals, a phenomenon the Solow model cannot capture

### 数据空白
- **Micro-level convergence**: The model's aggregate $k$ obscures firm-level heterogeneity. Hsieh and Klenow (2009) show that capital misallocation across firms within countries can reduce TFP by 30-50% in India and China
- **Intangible capital**: Modern economies invest heavily in intangibles (software, R&D, organizational capital) that depreciate differently from physical capital and are poorly measured in national accounts
- **Climate capital stranding**: The model assumes capital is always productive. With climate transition, fossil-fuel capital may become "stranded," invalidating the standard depreciation assumption
