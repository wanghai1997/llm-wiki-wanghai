# 新古典投资模型

The **neoclassical investment model**, formalized by [[Dale Jorgenson]] (1963), treats capital as a factor of production that firms "rent" in a hypothetical rental market. Investment is the process of adjusting the actual capital stock toward the profit-maximizing level, given interest rates, technology, and factor prices.

## The Rental Rate of Capital

A firm that rents a unit of capital at rate $R$ and uses it with labor to produce output maximizes:

$$\max_{K,L} \; pF(K,L) - WL - RK$$

The first-order condition for capital:

$$p \cdot MPK = R$$

The rental rate $R$ must cover the opportunity cost of owning capital:

$$R = P_K(r + \delta)$$

where $P_K$ is the price of capital, $r$ is the real interest rate, and $\delta$ is the depreciation rate.

**The user cost of capital**:

$$(r + \delta)$$

represents the total cost per dollar of capital: the real interest (foregone return on other assets) plus depreciation.

## The Desired Capital Stock

Setting $MPK = r + \delta$:

$$MPK = r + \delta$$

For Cobb-Douglas production $Y = K^\alpha L^{1-\alpha}$:

$$\alpha \frac{Y}{K} = r + \delta \implies K^* = \frac{\alpha Y}{r + \delta}$$

Desired capital $K^*$ depends:
- **Positively** on output $Y$ (higher production needs more capital)
- **Negatively** on $(r + \delta)$ (higher capital cost reduces optimal stock)

## From Desired Capital to Investment

Investment adjusts the actual capital stock toward the desired level:

$$I = K^* - K_{-1} + \delta K_{-1}$$

- **Net investment**: $K^* - K_{-1}$ (closing the gap)
- **Replacement investment**: $\delta K_{-1}$ (covering depreciation)

## With Convex Adjustment Costs

In reality, rapid capital adjustment is costly (installation, reorganization, learning). With quadratic adjustment costs:

$$C(I) = \frac{\psi}{2} \left( \frac{I}{K_{-1}} - \delta \right)^2 K_{-1}$$

Investment becomes a gradual process:

$$I/K = \delta + \frac{1}{\psi}(q - 1)$$

where $q$ is the shadow price of installed capital (see [[Tobin q理论]]). This bridges the neoclassical model to q theory.

## Implications for Aggregate Demand

The investment function is a key component of the IS curve:

$$I = I(r, Y, \text{expectations})$$

- $\partial I / \partial r < 0$: Higher rates reduce investment
- $\partial I / \partial Y > 0$: Higher output raises desired capital
- **Expectations**: Forward-looking firms respond to anticipated future profitability, not just current conditions

## 反面论点与数据空白

- **q理论的经验优于用户成本**: Empirically, stock market q predicts investment better than the user cost of capital $(r + \delta)$. This suggests expectations and financial market signals dominate mechanical cost calculations.
- **资本异质性**: The model treats capital as homogeneous. In reality, equipment, structures, software, and intellectual property have different depreciation rates, adjustment costs, and complementarities with labor.
- **不可逆性**: Capital, once installed, is often difficult to resell. This creates an option value of waiting—firms may delay investment when uncertainty is high, even if current conditions favor investment (Dixit-Pindyck 1994).
- **aggregate inertia from lumpiness**: Micro-level investment is lumpy (firms make discrete large adjustments). These do not cancel out in aggregate, creating persistence that the smooth neoclassical model cannot explain.
- **regional-bias**: The model assumes well-functioning rental markets for capital. In developing countries, where property rights are weak and financial markets underdeveloped, the concept of a rental rate is less meaningful.
