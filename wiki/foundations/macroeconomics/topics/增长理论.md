# 增长理论

**Growth theory** is the branch of macroeconomics that studies the determinants of long-run increases in per-capita income and standards of living. It addresses the most fundamental question in economics: why are some countries rich and others poor, and what policies can promote sustained prosperity?

## Historical Development

### The Pre-History: Classical Economics

Before modern growth theory, economists grappled with the limits to growth:
- **Adam Smith** (1776): Division of labor and capital accumulation drive growth, but diminishing returns eventually slow progress
- **[[Thomas Robert Malthus]]** (1798): Population growth inevitably outstrips food production, pinning living standards at subsistence
- **David Ricardo**: The "stationary state" where land scarcity halts growth
- **Karl Marx**: Capital accumulation drives up the organic composition of capital, reducing the rate of profit and eventually causing capitalist collapse

### The Neoclassical Revolution: Solow-Swan (1956)

The modern era of growth theory began with [[Robert Solow]] and [[Trevor Swan]] (1956), who introduced the [[Solow增长模型]]:
- A neoclassical production function with **diminishing returns to capital**
- **Capital accumulation** as the engine of growth
- An **exogenous** rate of technological progress as the ultimate driver of long-run per-capita growth
- The distinction between **level effects** (saving, population) and **growth effects** (technology)

The Solow model became the dominant framework, but its treatment of technology as "manna from heaven" remained unsatisfying.

### Growth Accounting: Solow (1957), Abramovitz (1956)

[[Moses Abramovitz]] and Solow developed [[增长核算与TFP]]—decomposing observed growth into factor accumulation and a residual (TFP). Abramovitz famously called TFP "a measure of our ignorance," highlighting how much remained unexplained.

### The Empirical Turn: Conditional Convergence and MRW (1992)

The [[Mankiw-Romer-Weil模型]] (1992) extended Solow by adding human capital, successfully explaining conditional convergence and ~80% of cross-country income variation. This established the augmented Solow framework as the empirical workhorse.

### Endogenous Growth: Romer (1986, 1990), Lucas (1988)

The "new growth theory" of the 1980s–90s endogenized technological progress:
- **[[Paul Romer]]** (1986, 1990): R&D produces nonrival ideas; scale effects and knowledge spillovers sustain growth
- **[[Robert Lucas Jr]]** (1988): Human capital accumulation with externalities
- **Aghion-Howitt** (1992): Schumpeterian creative destruction as the driver

These models made long-run growth rates depend on policy: education, R&D subsidies, patents, trade.

### Institutions and Deep Determinants (2000s–present)

The most recent wave emphasizes **institutions** as the fundamental determinant:
- **[[Daron Acemoglu]]**, Johnson, and Robinson (2001): Colonial origins of institutions explain modern income differences
- **Hall-Jones** (1999), **Klenow-Rodríguez-Clare** (1997): TFP/efficiency differences, not factor accumulation, explain most cross-country variation
- **Bloom-Van Reenen** (2007): Management practices as a micro-level determinant of TFP

## Key Empirical Regularities

### Kaldor's Stylized Facts

The [[平衡增长路径]] of the augmented Solow model reconciles [[Nicholas Kaldor]]'s six stylized facts:
1. Per-capita output grows steadily
2. Capital per worker grows steadily
3. Return on capital is stable
4. Capital-output ratio is stable
5. Factor shares are stable
6. Growth rates differ across countries

### Convergence Patterns

- **No absolute convergence** globally: poor countries do not automatically grow faster
- **Conditional convergence**: countries converge to their own steady states at ~2% per year
- **Convergence clubs**: convergence occurs within groups of similar countries
- See [[收敛假说]]

### Factor Accumulation vs Efficiency

Cross-country income differences are driven primarily by **TFP/efficiency differences** (~2/3) rather than factor accumulation (~1/3). See [[要素积累vs生产效率]].

## Policy Implications

### What Works (Broad Consensus)

1. **Education and human capital**: The MRW model and Lucas framework both emphasize schooling
2. **Health and nutrition**: Healthy workers are more productive
3. **Property rights and rule of law**: The deepest determinant in the institutional view
4. **Free trade and openness**: Technology diffusion, scale effects, competitive discipline
5. **R&D and innovation policy**: Subsidies, patents, university research (in endogenous growth models)

### What Is Contested

1. **Industrial policy**: Government picking winners vs market allocation
2. **Population policy**: Solow says lower $n$ raises $y^*$; Kremer says larger population drives innovation
3. **Income redistribution**: Does inequality help or hurt growth?
4. **Environmental constraints**: Is infinite growth feasible on a finite planet?

## Major Tensions and Unresolved Questions

### [CONTRADICTION-1] Level Effect vs Growth Effect
The saving rate raises the **level** of income but not its long-run **growth rate** in the Solow model. Yet transitional dynamics after policy changes create temporary growth bursts that are easily confused with permanent effects. See [[储蓄率与经济增长]].

### [CONTRADICTION-2] Three Population-Growth Theories
Solow, Malthus, and Kremer make opposite predictions about population and income. They apply to different historical regimes but are presented without clear boundaries. See [[人口增长与索洛模型]].

### [CONTRADICTION-3] Institutions: Causation or Correlation?
The AJR colonial-institutions framework is influential but contested. Geography, culture, and pre-colonial institutions may also matter. See [[制度与经济增长]].

### [CONTRADICTION-4] Free Trade Consensus Under Challenge
The textbook's confident endorsement of free trade ignores the 2010s deglobalization trend and the distributional costs of trade shocks. See [[促进增长的政策]].

### [BIAS-1] Neoclassical Victory Narrative
The textbook frames growth theory as Solow's triumph over Marx, erasing post-Keynesian and structuralist alternatives.

### [BIAS-2] American Case Dominance
Industrial policy examples are overwhelmingly American, ignoring East Asian success stories.

### [BIAS-3] Western Management Bias
The Bloom-Van Reenen survey's 4-country sample (US, UK, France, Germany) excludes East Asian manufacturing and emerging markets.

### [regional-bias] East Asian Miracle
The textbook downplays the Young-Krugman debate about whether East Asian growth is sustainable.

## 数据空白 (Beyond the Textbook)

1. **Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans optimal growth**: The textbook omits this foundational optimal savings framework
2. **Diamond OLG model**: The standard tool for analyzing dynamic inefficiency is absent
3. **2010s productivity slowdown**: Post-2008 TFP stagnation in advanced economies
4. **AI and digital economy**: GDP fails to capture free digital services; AI as GPT challenges productivity measurement
5. **Climate and growth**: Stern Review, carbon budgets, degrowth literature
6. **China's growth model**: High savings + high investment + institutional features beyond the Solow framework
