# Edward Prescott

**Edward Christian Prescott** (1940–2022) was an American economist at Arizona State University, awarded the **2004 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences** jointly with [[Finn Kydland]] "for their contributions to dynamic macroeconomics." Prescott co-founded the **Real Business Cycle (RBC)** paradigm with Kydland and independently made major contributions to growth theory (the "Prescott computational experiment") and taxation.

## Real Business Cycle Theory (1982)

Prescott's role in the Kydland-Prescott (1982) model was primarily in **methodology** and **quantitative implementation**:

### The "Computational Experiment"

Prescott advocated treating macroeconomics as an **experimental science**:
1. Build a fully specified dynamic general equilibrium model with optimizing agents
2. Calibrate parameters using long-run growth facts (not short-run business cycle data)
3. Feed in measured technology shocks (Solow residual)
4. Compare model-generated "data" (simulations) with actual business cycle statistics

**Calibration targets**:
- Capital share of income ≈ 1/3
- Investment/GDP ratio ≈ 1/4
- Average growth rate ≈ 2%
- Average return to capital ≈ 6-7%

### The RBC "Success"

Prescott claimed that the calibrated RBC model could explain **70% of US output fluctuations** using technology shocks alone. This was a bold claim that sparked decades of debate.

**Critique of the 70% figure**:
- The Solow residual is a **measurement** of unexplained productivity growth, not necessarily a true exogenous shock
- It includes demand shocks, measurement error, and variable factor utilization
- Correcting for variable utilization (Burnside-Eichenbaum-Rebelo 1993) reduces the explanatory power to ~30-50%

## The "Great Depression" as a Technology Shock? (1999)

In a controversial paper with Harold Cole, Prescott argued that the **Great Depression** could be explained by a severe negative technology shock. This was widely criticized:
- No identifiable major technological regression in the 1930s
- The banking panics and monetary contraction (Friedman-Schwartz) are more plausible causes
- Cole-Prescott required implausibly large TFP declines

This exemplified the RBC approach's tendency to **force all fluctuations into the technology-shock framework**, even when historical evidence suggests other causes.

## Taxes and Labor Supply (2004)

Prescott made a second major controversial claim: **differences in labor supply across countries are primarily due to tax differences**.

**The argument**: Europeans work fewer hours than Americans because marginal tax rates are higher in Europe, reducing the incentive to work.

**Evidence**: Prescott (2004) showed a strong correlation between marginal tax wedges and hours worked across OECD countries.

**Critique**:
- Ignores institutional differences (unions, mandated vacation, cultural norms)
- Ignores the composition of government spending (transfers vs. public goods)
- The US-Europe gap emerged primarily after 1970, but tax differences existed earlier
- Women's labor force participation (a major driver of hours differences) is poorly explained by taxes alone

## 反面论点与数据空白

- **RBC的方法论霸权**: Prescott将calibration提升为"科学方法",但批评者认为这实际上是逃避统计检验。模型参数设定主观,且模型对许多参数高度敏感。"不可证伪"是RBC最严重的弱点。
- **技术冲击的内生性**: Prescott假设Solow残差是外生冲击,但实证研究显示它高度内生于经济周期——企业在衰退期减少研发投入、产能利用率下降,这些都使TFP看起来"下降",但并非真正的技术退步。
- **"政策无效"的极端立场**: RBC隐含政策(货币/财政)对实际变量无影响,这与2008后大规模财政刺激和QE的经验直接矛盾。
- **regional-bias**: Prescott的跨国比较以OECD为样本,对发展中国家(制度不稳定、非正规就业、金融抑制)完全忽略。
