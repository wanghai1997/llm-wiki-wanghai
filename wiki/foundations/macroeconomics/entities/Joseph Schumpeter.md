# Joseph Schumpeter

**Joseph Alois Schumpeter** (1883–1950), Austrian-American economist and political scientist, is best known for his theory of **creative destruction** and his vision of capitalism as an evolutionary process driven by entrepreneurial innovation.

## Creative Destruction (1942)

In *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942), Schumpeter introduced the concept of **creative destruction** ("schöpferische Zerstörung"):

> "The opening up of new markets, foreign or domestic, and the organizational development from the craft shop... illustrate the same process of industrial mutation—if I may use that biological term—that incessantly revolutionizes the economic structure from within, incessantly destroying the old one, incessantly creating a new one. This process of Creative Destruction is the essential fact about capitalism."

Key elements:
- Innovation is not gradual improvement but **discontinuous leaps** that render existing technologies, firms, and skills obsolete
- **Entrepreneurs** (not ordinary managers or capitalists) are the agents of change, driven by the will to conquer and create kingdoms
- The competitive threat of new entrants disciplines incumbents more effectively than price competition among existing firms
- Creative destruction generates **growth but also turbulence**: unemployment, displaced workers, obsolete industries

## Contrast with Neoclassical Growth Theory

Schumpeter's vision differs fundamentally from the [[Solow增长模型]] and even from [[Paul Romer]]'s endogenous growth model:

| Dimension | Solow/Romer | Schumpeter |
|---|---|---|
| Growth driver | Smooth capital accumulation / steady R&D | Disruptive innovation by entrepreneurs |
| Role of existing firms | Passive recipients of technology | Obstacles to be overthrown |
| Welfare implications | Growth is uniformly beneficial | Growth involves destruction, displacement, and pain |
| Policy implication | Subsidize R&D, education | Protect entrepreneurship, tolerate disruption |

## Schumpeterian Growth Models

Modern formalizations of Schumpeterian ideas—most notably the **Aghion-Howitt model** (1992, 1998)—incorporate creative destruction into dynamic general equilibrium:
- Innovation is driven by **R&D races** where firms compete to discover the next generation of technology
- Successful innovators receive **temporary monopoly profits** until the next innovation destroys their advantage
- **"Business stealing" effect**: innovators do not internalize the profits they destroy for incumbents, leading to potentially excessive R&D
- Growth rate depends on the **frequency of innovations**, which is increasing in R&D labor but subject to diminishing returns

## In Mankiw's Textbook

Mankiw Ch 9-4 briefly presents the Schumpeterian/Aghion-Howitt framework as an alternative to the [[Paul Romer|Romer]] model, emphasizing:
- Innovation creates temporary monopoly power (patents)
- New technologies displace old ones (Luddites, Walmart vs. small retailers)
- European retail regulation as an example of policies that slow creative destruction

The textbook does not deeply explore the welfare tradeoffs of creative destruction or the empirical evidence on its importance relative to Romer-style knowledge accumulation.
