# Paul Samuelson

**Paul Anthony Samuelson** (1915–2009), MIT economist, was awarded the 1970 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences—the first American to receive the prize—"for the scientific work through which he has developed static and dynamic economic theory and actively contributed to raising the level of analysis in economic science."

## Core Contributions

### The Neoclassical Synthesis

Samuelson's 1947 *Foundations of Economic Analysis* established the mathematical rigor of modern economics. In macroeconomics, his 1955 textbook *Economics: An Introductory Analysis* (co-authored with William Nordhaus in later editions) codified the **neoclassical synthesis**:
- **Short run**: Keynesian analysis with sticky prices, where aggregate demand determines output and unemployment may persist
- **Long run**: Classical analysis with flexible prices, where the economy returns to full-employment equilibrium

This synthesis became the dominant paradigm in intermediate macroeconomics—including the very structure of Mankiw 9e (Part II-III = long-run classical; Part IV = short-run Keynesian).

### The Keynesian Cross and Multiplier-Accelerator Model

Samuelson formalized the [[凯恩斯交叉模型]] into the standard pedagogical tool for teaching equilibrium income determination. He also developed the **multiplier-accelerator model** (Hansen-Samuelson model), which showed how the interaction between the consumption multiplier and the investment accelerator could generate endogenous business cycles.

### Revealed Preference and Welfare Economics

Beyond macroeconomics, Samuelson's contributions to consumer theory (revealed preference), international trade (Stolper-Samuelson theorem, Factor Price Equalization), and public goods theory have shaped virtually every field of economics.

## Relationship to Mankiw's Framework

The architecture of Mankiw 9e—long-run classical followed by short-run Keynesian—is the direct intellectual descendant of Samuelson's neoclassical synthesis. The [[凯恩斯交叉模型]] Mankiw presents in Ch 11 is essentially Samuelson's formalization.

## Critical Assessment

The neoclassical synthesis was later challenged by:
- [[Milton Friedman]]'s monetarist critique (natural rate hypothesis)
- [[Robert Lucas Jr]]'s rational expectations critique (Lucas critique)
- New Classical and Real Business Cycle theorists who rejected the Keynesian short-run entirely

These critiques led to the New Keynesian synthesis, which attempted to provide microfoundations for price stickiness rather than assuming it ad hoc.
