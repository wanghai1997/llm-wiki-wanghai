# David Weil

**David N. Weil** (b. 1963), economist at Brown University, is best known as the coauthor (with [[N. Gregory Mankiw]] and [[Paul Romer]]) of the highly influential **Mankiw-Romer-Weil (MRW) 1992 paper**, which extended the [[Solow增长模型]] to include human capital and tested it against cross-country data.

## Mankiw-Romer-Weil (1992): Augmenting Solow with Human Capital

The paper *"A Contribution to the Empirics of Economic Growth"* (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1992) addressed a major empirical weakness of the basic Solow model: its prediction of **absolute convergence** (poor countries automatically growing faster than rich ones) was rejected by the data. The augmented model added human capital accumulation (proxied by school enrollment rates) as a third factor of production alongside physical capital and raw labor.

Key results:
- The augmented Solow model successfully explains about **80% of cross-country income variation**
- It predicts **conditional convergence**: countries converge to their own steady states, which differ based on savings, population growth, and education
- The estimated capital share (~1/3) and human capital share (~1/3) are consistent with micro evidence
- The model's implied rate of convergence (~2% per year) matches observed patterns

The MRW framework became the dominant empirical workhorse for cross-country growth analysis throughout the 1990s and 2000s, cited thousands of times.

## Subsequent Research

Weil's subsequent work has focused on:
- Demographic economics and the economic consequences of population change
- Health and economic growth (the "wealth of nations" through health improvements)
- Economic development in sub-Saharan Africa
- Macroeconomic textbooks: Weil is the coauthor (with Mankiw) of later editions of the *Macroeconomics* textbook

## Mankiw's Textbook Treatment

The textbook draws heavily on the MRW framework in Ch 9-2's empirical discussion, though it does not prominently name Weil as a coauthor of the underlying research. The conditional convergence result, the role of human capital, and the regression evidence all derive from the 1992 paper. Students reading the textbook benefit from knowing the intellectual provenance of the empirical claims.
