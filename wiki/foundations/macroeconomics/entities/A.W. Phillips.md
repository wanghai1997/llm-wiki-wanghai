# A.W. Phillips

**Alban William Housego Phillips** (1914–1975), New Zealand-born economist at the London School of Economics (LSE), is the originator of the **Phillips curve**—the empirical relationship between wage inflation and unemployment that became one of the most consequential and controversial relationships in macroeconomics.

## The Phillips Curve (1958)

In his landmark 1958 paper *"The Relation Between Unemployment and the Rate of Change of Money Wage Rates in the United Kingdom, 1861–1957"* (published in *Economica*), Phillips documented a striking inverse relationship:

- When unemployment was low, money wage rates tended to rise rapidly
- When unemployment was high, money wage rates grew slowly or even fell

Phillips fitted a curve to nearly a century of UK data, showing that the relationship was remarkably stable across different historical periods—including the Gold Standard, the interwar years, and the post-war era. The original curve related **wage inflation** (not price inflation) to unemployment.

## The Intellectual Impact

Phillips's empirical finding was quickly adopted by Keynesian economists as evidence of a **stable, exploitable tradeoff** between inflation and unemployment. The policy implication seemed clear: governments could choose their preferred point on the Phillips curve—accepting higher inflation for lower unemployment, or vice versa.

This interpretation dominated macroeconomic policymaking throughout the 1960s, influencing central banks and treasuries worldwide.

## The Collapse of the Original Phillips Curve

The stable tradeoff broke down in the late 1960s and 1970s:
- **Stagflation**: The simultaneous occurrence of high inflation and high unemployment (impossible under the original Phillips curve)
- **[[Milton Friedman]]** (1968) and **[[Edmund Phelps]]** (1967–68) independently showed that the tradeoff was only **short-run**; in the long run, the Phillips curve was **vertical** at the natural rate of unemployment
- **[[Robert Lucas Jr]]** (1972) delivered the theoretical coup de grâce: under rational expectations, even the short-run tradeoff disappeared if policy was anticipated

## Legacy

Despite its empirical demise, the Phillips curve lives on in modified forms:
- **Expectations-augmented Phillips curve**: $\pi = \pi^e - \beta(u - u^n)$, incorporating expected inflation
- **New Keynesian Phillips curve**: Forward-looking, microfounded version used in modern DSGE models
- **Empirical Phillips curve**: Central banks still use output-gap-based inflation forecasts that are essentially Phillips curves in disguise

The Phillips curve thus represents a case study in the tension between empirical regularity and theoretical foundation in economics—a relationship that "works" until it doesn't, revealing deeper structural forces beneath the surface correlation.
