# Michael Kremer

**Michael Robert Kremer** (b. 1964), University Professor at the University of Chicago (formerly Harvard), was awarded the 2019 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (shared with [[Esther Duflo]] and [[Abhijit Banerjee]]) for his experimental approach to alleviating global poverty. Within macroeconomics, he is known for a distinctive model in which **population size drives technological progress**.

## The Kremerian Model (1993)

In *"Population Growth and Technological Change: One Million B.C. to 1990"* (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1993), Kremer proposed that technological progress is an increasing function of **population size** rather than being exogenous (as in [[Solow增长模型]]) or driven by R&D investment per se (as in [[Paul Romer]]'s model).

### Core Mechanism

The model rests on two key assumptions:
1. **More people → more ideas**: Technological advancement is proportional to population because more people means more potential innovators, researchers, and problem-solvers
2. **Technology raises carrying capacity**: Better technology (agriculture, medicine, organization) allows larger populations to be sustained

This creates a **positive feedback loop**:
- Larger population → faster technological progress
- Faster technological progress → higher output per person (in the long run)
- Higher output → larger population (until constrained by carrying capacity)

### Historical Implications

Kremer tested the model against long-run historical data (1 million B.C. to 1990):
- Population growth was extremely slow for most of human history (consistent with Malthusian constraints)
- As world population grew, the *rate* of population growth itself accelerated
- This acceleration is consistent with the feedback loop between population and technology
- The model explains why the "takeoff" to modern economic growth coincided with rapid population expansion

## Tension with Competing Frameworks

Mankiw's textbook (Ch 8-3) presents three incompatible views of population and growth without clearly demarcating their historical domains:

| Model | Population→Income | Historical Domain |
|---|---|---|
| **Solow** | Higher n → lower steady-state k* and y* | Industrial/post-industrial economies with given technology |
| **Malthus** | Population growth keeps income at subsistence | Pre-industrial agrarian economies |
| **Kremer** | Larger population → faster tech progress → higher income | Very long run; global scale; possibly modern innovation clusters |

The textbook does not resolve when each model applies, leaving a significant conceptual gap. See [[人口增长与索洛模型]] for an analysis of this tension.
