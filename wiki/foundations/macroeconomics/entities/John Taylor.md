# John Taylor

**John Brian Taylor** (born 1946), economist at Stanford University, is one of the most influential monetary economists of his generation. He was awarded the 2024 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (shared with Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson) "for studies of how institutions are formed and affect prosperity." Taylor's most famous contribution is the **Taylor rule** (1993)—a simple formula for setting interest rates that has become the benchmark for central bank policy analysis worldwide.

## Core Contributions

### The Taylor Rule (1993)

In his 1993 paper *"Discretion Versus Policy Rules in Practice,"* Taylor proposed a simple interest rate rule:

$$i = r^* + \pi + 0.5(\pi - \pi^*) + 0.5(y - \bar{y})$$

Where:
- $i$ = nominal federal funds rate
- $r^*$ = equilibrium real interest rate (~2%)
- $\pi$ = current inflation rate
- $\pi^*$ = target inflation rate (~2%)
- $y - \bar{y}$ = output gap (percentage deviation from potential)

The rule captures two key principles of modern monetary policy:
1. **Respond to inflation**: If inflation rises above target, raise rates more than one-for-one (Taylor principle)
2. **Respond to output**: If the economy overheats (positive output gap), raise rates; if it slumps, lower them

Taylor showed that this simple rule described Fed behavior remarkably well from 1987 to 1992, and it has since become the standard benchmark for evaluating monetary policy.

### Staggered Wage and Price Contracts

Before the Taylor rule, Taylor made fundamental contributions to **New Keynesian microfoundations**:
- **Taylor (1980)**: Developed a model of overlapping ("staggered") wage contracts, showing how nominal rigidities could arise from rational, optimizing behavior
- In Taylor's model, firms set prices for fixed durations, with only a fraction adjusting each period
- This created persistent nominal rigidities without relying on ad hoc assumptions

Taylor's staggered contracts model was an important predecessor to the **Calvo pricing** model that became standard in New Keynesian DSGE.

### Taylor Principle and Monetary Stability

Taylor emphasized that for monetary policy to stabilize inflation, the central bank must raise nominal interest rates **more than one-for-one** with inflation:
- If $\partial i / \partial \pi > 1$, real rates rise when inflation rises, dampening demand
- If $\partial i / \partial \pi < 1$, real rates fall when inflation rises, destabilizing the economy

This "Taylor principle" is a necessary condition for determinate, non-explosive equilibria in monetary models.

### Policy Debates

Taylor has been an active participant in monetary policy debates:
- **Critic of QE**: Argued that the Fed's large-scale asset purchases after 2008 departed from rules-based policy
- **Advocate of rules over discretion**: Has consistently argued that predictable, rule-based policy is superior to discretionary fine-tuning
- **Fed accountability**: Proposed legislation (the "Taylor Rule Act") requiring the Fed to explain deviations from a benchmark rule

## Relationship to Mankiw's Framework

Mankiw 9e does not explicitly name Taylor in the main text of Ch 14, but the entire discussion of modern monetary policy implicitly relies on the Taylor rule framework:
- The tradeoff between inflation and output gap (the modern Phillips curve)
- The central bank's response function (raising rates when inflation rises)
- The distinction between systematic and discretionary policy

Taylor's staggered contracts model also underlies the New Keynesian explanation for price stickiness that Mankiw presents.
