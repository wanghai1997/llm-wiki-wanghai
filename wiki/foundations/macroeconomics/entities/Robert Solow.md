# Robert Solow

**Robert Merton Solow** (1924–2023), MIT economist, awarded the 1987 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences "for his contributions to the theory of economic growth."

## Core Contribution: The Solow Growth Model (1956)

In a landmark 1956 paper *"A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth"* (Quarterly Journal of Economics), Solow constructed the foundational neoclassical growth model that bears his name. The model's central innovation was to embed a production function with diminishing returns to capital into a dynamic framework of capital accumulation, demonstrating that economies converge to a **steady state** where per-capita income growth ceases unless driven by exogenous technological progress.

Key elements of the Solow model:
- Aggregate production function $Y = F(K, L)$ with constant returns to scale
- Per-worker form $y = f(k)$ exhibiting diminishing marginal product of capital (MPK)
- Capital accumulation equation: $\Delta k = sf(k) - \delta k$
- Steady-state condition: $sf(k^*) = \delta k^*$
- Prediction that the saving rate affects the *level* of per-capita income but not its long-run *growth rate*

Solow independently developed the model around the same time as Australian economist [[Trevor Swan]], leading to the occasional joint designation "Solow-Swan model" in the literature.

## The Solow Residual and Growth Accounting

In a 1957 follow-up paper, Solow pioneered **growth accounting**, decomposing output growth into contributions from capital accumulation, labor force growth, and a residual term—later termed **total factor productivity (TFP)** or the "Solow residual"—that captures technological progress and all other unexplained sources of growth. This decomposition became the standard empirical framework for analyzing growth sources across countries and time periods.

## Relationship to Subsequent Research

The Solow model's treatment of technology as purely **exogenous** (falling like "manna from heaven") became the focal point of later critiques, most notably from [[Paul Romer]], whose endogenous growth theory (Romer 1990) explicitly modeled the production of new knowledge and ideas. This intellectual trajectory—from Solow's exogenous framework to Romer's endogenous innovation—defines the central narrative arc of modern growth theory.

## In Mankiw's Textbook

Mankiw Ch 8 builds the entire Solow model from first principles, using it to analyze capital accumulation, population growth, and steady-state dynamics. Ch 9 extends the framework to incorporate technological progress and confronts the model with empirical evidence on cross-country income differences and convergence.
