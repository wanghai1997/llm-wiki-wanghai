# Thomas Robert Malthus

**Thomas Robert Malthus** (1766–1834), English cleric and scholar, published *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798), one of the most influential—and most famously wrong—works in the history of economic thought.

## The Malthusian Model

Malthus's core argument:
1. **Food production** grows arithmetically (linearly): each additional unit of land yields diminishing returns
2. **Population** grows geometrically (exponentially): unchecked, human populations double every generation
3. **Result**: Population growth inevitably outstrips food supply, driving per-capita income down to **subsistence level**
4. **"Positive checks"**: Famine, disease, and war periodically reduce population, temporarily raising living standards above subsistence—only to trigger renewed population growth that drives income back down

The Malthusian trap implies that sustained improvements in living standards are impossible without some form of **preventative check** on reproduction (which Malthus, as a cleric, opposed in the form of contraception).

## Why Malthus Was Wrong—for Industrial Economies

The Industrial Revolution broke the Malthusian trap through:
- **Technological progress** growing faster than population (contradicting Malthus's assumption of arithmetic food growth)
- **The demographic transition**: rising incomes eventually led to *lower* fertility as education increased and child mortality fell
- **Capital accumulation** raising output per worker independently of land constraints

However, Malthus was arguably **descriptively accurate for most of human history** (pre-1800). The world economy was in a Malthusian equilibrium for millennia, with technological improvements mainly absorbed by population growth rather than per-capita income gains. This "Malthusian era" is now a standard phase in unified growth theory.

## In Mankiw's Textbook

Mankiw Ch 8-3 uses the Malthusian model as a contrast to the [[Solow增长模型]]. Where Solow predicts that higher population growth lowers steady-state per-capita income but still permits positive living standards, Malthus predicts population growth drives income to biological subsistence. The textbook notes that "Malthus's dark predictions have not come to pass" for modern economies but does not explore the model's historical accuracy for pre-industrial societies.

The textbook also juxtaposes Malthus with [[Michael Kremer]]'s opposing model (where population drives technological progress), without explaining when each applies. See [[人口增长与索洛模型]] for a resolution of this conceptual tension.
