# Alwyn Young

**Alwyn Young** (b. 1962), economist at the Chicago Booth School of Business, is best known for his growth accounting analyses of the East Asian economies that ignited a major debate about the nature of the "East Asian miracle."

## The East Asian Growth Decomposition (1992, 1995)

In two influential papers—*"A Tale of Two Cities"* (1992) on Hong Kong and Singapore, and *"The Tyranny of Numbers"* (1995) on the East Asian NICs (Newly Industrialized Countries) more broadly—Young applied rigorous growth accounting to decompose the rapid output growth of East Asian economies into contributions from factor accumulation (capital deepening, labor force expansion, human capital growth) versus TFP growth.

His striking finding: most East Asian growth could be explained by **exceptionally high rates of factor accumulation**—particularly physical and human capital investment—rather than by rapid TFP growth. For Singapore, TFP growth was near zero; for South Korea and Taiwan, it was positive but modest relative to the headline growth rates.

## The Policy Debate

Young's findings were amplified by [[Paul Krugman]]'s 1994 article *"The Myth of Asia's Miracle"* (Foreign Affairs), which argued that East Asian growth was largely "perspiration" (factor accumulation) rather than "inspiration" (productivity improvement), and was therefore subject to diminishing returns and potentially unsustainable—analogous to Soviet growth in the 1950s–60s.

This debate had major policy implications:
- **If Young/Krugman were right**: East Asian growth would slow as returns to capital diminish; the "miracle" was a one-time catch-up via mobilization of inputs
- **If critics were right** (e.g., those emphasizing measurement issues, sectoral reallocation, or learning-by-doing): TFP was underestimated and growth more sustainable

## Mankiw's Treatment

Mankiw's textbook (Ch 9-2) mentions the finding that "international variation in standards of living is attributable to a combination of capital accumulation and the efficiency with which capital is used" but does not prominently feature the Young-Krugman debate or its policy implications for the sustainability of the East Asian model. This elision downplays an important empirical controversy. See [[宏观经济学foundations总览]] §3.3 on the "regional-bias" in the textbook's treatment of the East Asian miracle.
