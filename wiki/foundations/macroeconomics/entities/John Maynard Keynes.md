# John Maynard Keynes

**John Maynard Keynes** (1883–1946), British economist at Cambridge University, is widely regarded as the founder of modern macroeconomics as a distinct field. His 1936 magnum opus *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* revolutionized economic thought by shifting the focus from supply-side determination of output to **aggregate demand** as the short-run driver of economic activity.

## Core Contribution: The General Theory (1936)

Before Keynes, the dominant classical paradigm held that flexible wages and prices would automatically restore full employment. Keynes challenged this view on both theoretical and empirical grounds:

- **Effective demand**: Output is determined not by productive capacity but by the sum of consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports
- **Liquidity preference**: The demand for money depends on income and interest rates, creating the possibility that monetary policy may fail at very low interest rates
- **Animal spirits**: Investment decisions are driven by unpredictable psychological factors, making private demand inherently unstable
- **Wage rigidity**: Even if workers accept nominal wage cuts, this would reduce aggregate demand via the paradox of thrift, deepening rather than curing recessions

## The Keynesian Revolution and IS-LM

Keynes's ideas were formalized into the [[IS-LM模型]] by [[John Hicks]] (1937) and popularized in the United States by [[Alvin Hansen]] and [[Paul Samuelson]]. This "neoclassical synthesis" became the standard macroeconomic framework from the 1940s through the 1960s, informing New Deal fiscal policy and post-war demand management.

## Relationship to Mankiw's Framework

Mankiw 9e presents Keynes as the intellectual origin of Part IV (short-run fluctuations). The [[AD-AS模型]], [[IS-LM模型]], and Mundell-Fleming model all trace their lineage to the General Theory's insistence that **prices are sticky in the short run** and **aggregate demand matters**.

## Critical Assessment

Mankiw's presentation of Keynes is largely technical—focusing on the IS-LM apparatus and multiplier mechanics—while downplaying:
- Keynes's critique of financial markets and speculation (Chapter 12 of the General Theory)
- The radical uncertainty (*fundamental uncertainty*) that pervades investment decisions
- The social and institutional dimensions of capitalist instability later developed by Post-Keynesians
