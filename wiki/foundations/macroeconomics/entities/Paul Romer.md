# Paul Romer

**Paul Michael Romer** (b. 1955), University Professor at NYU Stern School of Business, awarded the 2018 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (shared with [[William Nordhaus]]) "for integrating technological innovations into long-run macroeconomic analysis."

## From Exogenous to Endogenous Growth

Romer's central contribution was to move technological progress from an **exogenous** black box (as in the [[Solow增长模型]]) to an **endogenous** outcome of intentional economic decisions, particularly research and development (R&D) investment.

### Romer (1986): Externalities and Increasing Returns

Romer's first growth paper (*"Increasing Returns and Long-Run Growth,"* JPE 1986) showed that knowledge spillovers—where one firm's innovation raises the productivity of all firms—can generate sustained per-capita growth even without exogenous technological progress. This established the possibility of "scale effects" where larger populations support faster growth.

### Romer (1990): R&D-Based Endogenous Growth

The canonical Romer model (*"Endogenous Technological Change,"* JPE 1990) introduced a three-sector economy:
1. **Final goods production**: Uses labor, capital, and designs/blueprints
2. **Intermediate goods**: Monopolistically competitive firms turn designs into specialized capital goods
3. **R&D sector**: Devotes human capital to producing new designs

Key features:
- **Ideas are nonrival**: Once created, a design can be used by many firms simultaneously at zero marginal cost
- **Partial excludability**: Patents grant temporary monopoly power, creating private incentives for R&D
- **"Standing on shoulders" effect**: New knowledge builds on existing knowledge (research productivity rises with the stock of knowledge)

The model predicts that long-run growth rates depend on policy variables: subsidies to R&D, education investment, and patent protection.

## Policy Implications

Romer's framework implies that growth-promoting policies should target the **production of ideas** rather than merely capital accumulation:
- R&D tax credits and direct subsidies
- Education and human capital formation
- Intellectual property regimes that balance incentive creation against access costs
- Openness to trade and foreign direct investment (which facilitates knowledge diffusion)

## Tension with the Solow Paradigm

Romer's work resolves the most unsatisfying feature of the Solow model—technology as "manna from heaven"—but introduces its own complexities: scale effects that may be too strong empirically, the difficulty of calibrating "knowledge production functions," and the political economy of determining which ideas receive public support.

## In Mankiw's Textbook

Mankiw Ch 9-4 presents Romer's model as the culmination of growth theory, contrasting it with the AK model and the Schumpeterian/Aghion-Howitt framework of creative destruction. The textbook emphasizes Romer's insight that ideas are nonrival goods and that this nonrivalry is the fundamental reason why market economies can sustain perpetual growth.
