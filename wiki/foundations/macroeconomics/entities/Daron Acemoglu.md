# Daron Acemoglu

**Daron Acemoglu** (b. 1967), Institute Professor at MIT, is one of the most influential economists of the early 21st century, known for his work on the institutional determinants of long-run economic development.

## The Colonial Origins of Institutions (Acemoglu-Johnson-Robinson, 2001)

The paper *"The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development"* (American Economic Review, 2001), coauthored with Simon Johnson and James A. Robinson (the "AJR" paper), proposed that the single most important determinant of modern cross-country income differences is **the quality of institutions**—property rights, rule of law, constraints on executive power—rather than geography, culture, or factor endowments.

### The Argument

1. **European colonization created institutional variation**: In regions where Europeans faced high mortality (tropical Africa, Central America), they established **extractive institutions** designed to exploit resources with minimal settlement. In regions with lower mortality (North America, Australasia), they established **settler institutions** resembling European property-rights frameworks.

2. **Institutional persistence**: These initial institutional choices persisted long after independence. Extractive colonies remained extractive; settler colonies maintained inclusive institutions.

3. **Causal effect**: Using **settler mortality rates** as an instrumental variable for institutional quality, AJR estimated that institutions explain the bulk of cross-country income differences.

### Mankiw's Treatment

Mankiw's textbook (Ch 9-3 Case Study, "The Colonial Origins of Modern Institutions") prominently features AJR 2001 as the explanation for why some nations are rich and others poor. The textbook summarizes the "remarkable correlation" between settler mortality and modern income, interpreting it as evidence for the primacy of institutions.

### Ongoing Debates and Controversies

The AJR framework, while enormously influential, faces significant methodological challenges that the textbook does not discuss:

- **Albouy (2012)**: Reconstructed the settler mortality data, finding that correcting measurement errors weakens the first-stage relationship and reduces the estimated effect of institutions
- **Sachs (2003) and geographic determinism**: Geography (malaria, soil quality, landlockedness) directly affects health and productivity, and may explain income differences independently of institutions. The AJR instrument may capture geographic factors as well as institutional ones
- **Measurement of institutions**: "Institutional quality" is a broad concept; different dimensions (property rights, democracy, regulatory efficiency) have different effects and may be endogenous to income itself
- **Asian development exceptions**: South Korea and Taiwan developed rapidly under authoritarian regimes with imperfect institutions, challenging a simplistic "good institutions → growth" causal chain

The textbook's selective presentation of AJR as settled science, without noting these ongoing debates, constitutes a significant [CONTRADICTION] and [BIAS]. See [[制度与经济增长]] for a fuller discussion.

## Broader Contributions

Beyond AJR 2001, Acemoglu's research spans:
- Political economy of development (why elites sometimes block growth-enhancing reforms)
- Directed technical change (technology biased toward skilled labor)
- Automation, AI, and the future of work
- Networks and economic outcomes
