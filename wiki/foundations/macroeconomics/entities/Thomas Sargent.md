# Thomas Sargent

**Thomas John Sargent** (born 1943), economist at New York University and the Hoover Institution, was awarded the 2011 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (shared with [[Christopher Sims]]) "for their empirical research on cause and effect in the macroeconomy." Sargent is a founding figure of the **rational expectations revolution** and a pioneer in the econometric implementation of dynamic macroeconomic models.

## Core Contributions

### Rational Expectations Macroeconometrics

Together with [[Robert Lucas Jr]], [[Neil Wallace]], and others, Sargent developed the theoretical and empirical tools for analyzing macroeconomic policy under **rational expectations**:
- Agents use all available information, including the structure of the economy and the policy regime
- Systematic policy changes are anticipated and neutralized in their real effects
- Only unanticipated ("surprise") policy can have real effects

### "The Ends of Four Big Inflations" (1982)

Sargent's most celebrated applied work analyzed the hyperinflations of Austria, Hungary, Poland, and Germany after World War I. His central finding:
- **Credible regime changes** could end hyperinflation almost overnight, without the massive output losses predicted by traditional Phillips curve analysis
- When governments credibly committed to fiscal discipline and monetary stability, inflation expectations adjusted rapidly
- The key ingredient was **credibility**, not gradualism

This work provided the empirical anchor for the "cold turkey" approach to disinflation—arguing that a swift, credible policy change was preferable to gradual tightening.

### Dynamic Macroeconomic Theory

Sargent's textbooks and research papers established the mathematical framework for modern macroeconomics:
- Recursive methods in dynamic economic models
- Bayesian estimation of DSGE models
- Learning dynamics (how agents update expectations when the true model is unknown)
- Robust control (policy design under model uncertainty)

### Learning and Bounded Rationality

In later work, Sargent relaxed the strict rational expectations assumption to study **adaptive learning**: what happens when agents don't know the true model but try to learn it from data? This work bridged the gap between rational expectations and more behavioral approaches.

## Relationship to Mankiw's Framework

Mankiw 9e Ch 14-2 presents Sargent's "Four Big Inflations" as the empirical case for **costless disinflation** under rational expectations and credible policy regimes. However, Mankiw also notes that the Volcker disinflation in the United States (1979–1982) was accompanied by a severe recession—suggesting that credibility and institutional context matter enormously.

## Critical Assessment

The rational expectations claim of costless disinflation has been controversial:
- **Volcker's experience**: US unemployment reached 10.8% in 1982—a massive output cost despite the Fed's credible commitment
- **European austerity 2010–2015**: Deep recessions followed fiscal consolidation, even when markets believed the commitments
- **Credibility is endogenous**: Governments that can credibly commit are rare; the historical cases Sargent studied involved extreme crisis conditions

Sargent's work remains foundational, but its policy implications are more nuanced than a simple "credibility → no cost" formula.
