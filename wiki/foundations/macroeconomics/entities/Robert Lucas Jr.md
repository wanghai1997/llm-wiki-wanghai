# Robert Lucas Jr.

**Robert Emerson Lucas Jr.** (1937–2023), University of Chicago economist, awarded the 1995 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences "for having developed and applied the hypothesis of rational expectations, and thereby having transformed macroeconomic analysis and deepened our understanding of economic policy."

## Contributions to Growth Theory

While Lucas is most famous for the **rational expectations revolution** and the **Lucas critique** of econometric policy evaluation, he also made fundamental contributions to growth economics.

### Lucas (1988): Human Capital and Growth

In *"On the Mechanics of Economic Development"* (Journal of Monetary Economics, 1988), Lucas extended the neoclassical growth framework by modeling human capital accumulation as an engine of growth. Key insights:
- Human capital (skills, knowledge) is accumulated through deliberate investment (education, on-the-job training)
- **External effects**: One worker's human capital raises the productivity of others (spillovers through interaction)
- With sufficiently strong externalities, human capital accumulation can sustain perpetual growth even without exogenous technological progress

This paper helped bridge the gap between the [[Solow增长模型]] and fully endogenous growth models like [[Paul Romer]]'s, by showing how an augmented factor accumulation mechanism could generate sustained growth.

### Lucas's Growth Puzzle

Lucas famously posed the question that motivated much of 1990s growth empirics: "Is there some action a government of India could take that would lead the Indian economy to grow like Indonesia's or Egypt's? If so, what, exactly? If not, what is it about the 'nature of India' that makes it so?" This framing motivated the cross-country regression literature, including the influential [[Mankiw-Romer-Weil模型]] (1992), which attempted to identify specific policy-levers (savings, education, population growth) that explain income differences.

## Contributions to Business Cycle Theory and Aggregate Supply

While Lucas's growth work is foundational, his most famous contributions are to business cycle theory—covered extensively in Mankiw 9e Ch 14.

### Lucas (1972): Rational Expectations and the Phillips Curve

In *"Expectations and the Neutrality of Money"* (Journal of Economic Theory, 1972), Lucas developed the [[不完全信息理论]] of aggregate supply. Key insights:
- Producers confuse aggregate price level changes with relative price changes
- Under [[理性预期]], only **unanticipated** monetary policy affects real output
- Systematic, predictable demand management is **ineffective**
- Provided a microfoundation for the Phillips curve without nominal rigidities

This paper launched the **rational expectations revolution** and fundamentally challenged Keynesian demand management. See [[理性预期]] / [[不完全信息理论]].

### Lucas (1976): The Lucas Critique

In *"Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique"* (Carnegie-Rochester Conference Series, 1976), Lucas delivered a methodological bombshell:
- Traditional econometric models cannot evaluate policy changes because their parameters depend on the policy regime
- The Phillips curve "tradeoff" is not structural—it shifts when policy changes
- This forced macroeconomics to adopt explicit microfoundations (preferences, technology) that are invariant to policy

The Lucas critique directly motivated the development of DSGE models and transformed how central banks conduct policy analysis. See [[Lucas批判]].

### Lucas Supply Function

Lucas derived the aggregate supply relationship:

$$y = \bar{y} + \gamma(P - P^e)$$

where $P^e$ is the rationally expected price level. This looks identical to the sticky-price SRAS, but the mechanism is entirely different: **no nominal rigidities**, only information imperfections. All three theories of aggregate supply (sticky wages, sticky prices, imperfect information) converge on the same reduced form. See [[总供给的微观基础]].

## Relationship to Mankiw's Textbook

Mankiw quotes Lucas's 1988 paper in the opening of Ch 9: "Once one starts to think about [economic growth], it is hard to think about anything else." This reflects Lucas's influence in elevating growth theory from a specialized topic to a central concern of macroeconomics.

In Ch 14, Lucas dominates the theoretical narrative:
- **Ch 14-1**: The imperfect information theory is presented as one of three microfoundations for SRAS
- **Ch 14-2**: The rational expectations critique of the Phillips curve and the Lucas critique are central to the chapter's intellectual history
- However, Mankiw notes that modern New Keynesian models (which Mankiw himself helped develop) reintroduced nominal rigidities while preserving Lucas's demand for microfoundations—synthesizing rather than rejecting Lucas's critique
